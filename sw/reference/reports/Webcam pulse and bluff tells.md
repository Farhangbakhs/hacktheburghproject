# Measure the pulse, not the lie

A Windows laptop webcam running Python, OpenCV and NumPy can do two things well enough for a live demo. It can make the pulse visible with Eulerian video magnification (EVM), and it can estimate heart rate with remote photoplethysmography (rPPG). It cannot detect bluffs. For EVM, the published recipe is heavy spatial pooling followed by a narrow temporal bandpass and an alpha of about 50. The proposed difference-of-two-lowpass IIR filter works, but at fs = 30 Hz its peak gain is only about 0.41 and its band is far wider than its nominal 0.83–2.0 Hz. You also need to pool more coarsely than 160x120 (go to pyramid level 3–4), lock exposure, and gate on motion. For heart rate, the strongest unsupervised choice is POS: a 1.6 s window, detrending, a 0.75–2.5 Hz Butterworth bandpass, and a 10 s zero-padded FFT with an SNR gate. It reaches about **4 bpm MAE on clean lab datasets and 8–12 bpm in realistic conditions**. The scientific literature is blunt about deception. Behavioural cues are faint (median |d| about 0.10), people detect lies at about 54%, and the US National Research Council concluded that physiological measures capture arousal, which has many causes, not deception. In poker specifically, bluffs and strong-hand bets produce similar arousal. So the honest product is a baseline-relative "arousal meter" with a visible signal-quality indicator. Turning that limitation into the demo's message ("arousal is a clue, not a verdict") makes the science stronger, not weaker.

## Pool harder before you amplify: EVM parameters that actually reveal a pulse

Wu et al.'s SIGGRAPH 2012 face results did not use the real-time IIR filter. For the face videos they used a Laplacian pyramid with the finest two levels zeroed, an **ideal (DCT/FFT) bandpass of 0.83–1.0 Hz**, alpha of about 100–120 and chroma weight λc of about 1000 at 30 fps ([Wu et al. 2012](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mrub/papers/vidmag.pdf)). The released result files show **alpha 50 on a Gaussian downsample to level 4 (face) or level 6 (face2)**, with no chroma attenuation. The project page describes 0.5–3 Hz as a "safe" heart-rate range and tells you to account for the frame rate ([MIT EVM project page](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mrub/evm/)). Spatial pooling is the step that matters. In the paper's noise experiment, a binomial filter of size 20 hid the pulse and one of size 80 revealed it. The authors state that "if the spatial filter applied is not large enough, the signal of interest will not be revealed," and that Eulerian error grows quadratically with alpha ([Wu et al. 2012](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mrub/papers/vidmag.pdf)). The darker-complexion subject (face2) was processed at a coarser level (6 vs 4) and a higher alpha (120 vs 100), which shows how to compensate for lower SNR.

This has a direct consequence for the planned pipeline. **On a 640x480 webcam, 160x120 is only pyramid level 2**, which is much less pooling than MIT used. Each extra level averages four times as many pixels and roughly halves the noise standard deviation. If the blush is not visible, the first fix is to drop to 80x60 or 40x30, not to raise alpha. cv2.pyrDown already uses the paper's 5-tap binomial kernel. Widening MIT's subject-tuned 0.83–1.0 Hz band to 0.83–2.0 Hz (50–120 bpm) is reasonable for an unknown live subject. Keep the chroma rather than attenuating it, because the red/green alternation is what makes the effect read as blood flow. MIT also restricted magnification to hand-marked face regions, because amplifying everything else looks bad ([Wu et al. 2012](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mrub/papers/vidmag.pdf)). A soft, feathered face mask gives the same effect.

### The DoLP filter is about 2.4x weaker and much wider than its cutoffs suggest

The paper itself proposes building the bandpass from two first-order lowpass IIR filters as the real-time option. MIT's own real-time C++ app, which ran 640x480 at 45 fps on a 2012 laptop, used a second-order Butterworth instead ([Wu et al. 2012](https://people.csail.mit.edu/mrub/papers/vidmag.pdf)). The figures below were computed from the filter equations for these notes; no outside source reports them. With fc = 0.83 and 2.0 Hz at fs = 30, the coefficients are r_lo = 0.1596 and r_hi = 0.3422. The peak gain is **0.41 at about 1.3 Hz**, the -3 dB band runs from **0.50 to 3.42 Hz**, and about 14% gain remains at 10 Hz. Effective amplification is therefore about 0.41·alpha, and sensor noise and aliased flicker at 5–15 Hz still get roughly 7–10x at alpha 50. The upside is a small phase shift across the pulse band (+27° at 50 bpm, -12° at 120 bpm), two state frames of memory, and no oscillatory ringing. A step such as a head move or exposure jump produces a decaying "ghost" that peaks at 0.31 of the step and is gone in about 26 frames (0.9 s).

| Choice | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Pyramid level | Level 3–4 (80x60 to 40x30) from 640x480; level 2 only if the light is very good |
| Temporal filter | DoLP 0.83/2.0 Hz is acceptable. For steeper skirts, use `scipy.signal.butter(1,[0.83,2.0],'band',fs=fs)` as a per-pixel biquad, or cascade the DoLP twice |
| Alpha | Start at 50 (about 20x real gain with DoLP). Slider range 20–150; lower alpha plus coarser level beats high alpha |
| Coefficients | Recompute r = 1 − exp(−2π·fc·dt) every frame from measured dt |
| Startup | Initialise both lowpass states to the first downsampled frame; ramp alpha in over about 30 frames |
| Output | float32 throughout; clip the amplified delta to ±20–40 levels; mask to the face; clip to [0,255] only at display |
| Motion | If mean inter-frame difference in the face ROI exceeds a threshold, reset the states to the current frame and ramp alpha back up |

There is no maintained, real-time Python webcam EVM to copy. The live apps (tschnz, MattW2) are C++/Qt ([tschnz/Live-Video-Magnification](https://www.GitHub.com/tschnz/Live-Video-Magnification)). The pip package `eulerian-magnification` is marked "not in a working state" by its author ([brycedrennan](https://www.github.com/brycedrennan/eulerian-magnification)). A Python project that tried per-pixel FFT filtering on a live webcam found it "too computationally expensive" ([kevroy314/respmon](https://github.com/kevroy314/respmon)). That leaves the 40-line IIR loop as the practical route. At 40x30 to 160x120 the per-pixel work is negligible, so capture and the full-resolution pyrUp/add dominate. This is an estimate, so time each stage on the target laptop. Handle non-power-of-two sizes with a final `cv2.resize` or by cropping to multiples of 2^levels. Integer-only state is a known risk if the filter is ever ported to fixed point. The pulse delta is below one 8-bit LSB, so the slow filter's update rounds to zero unless the state carries fractional bits.

## Windows webcams break the filter before the algorithm does

The most likely way the demo fails is the camera, not the maths. Auto-exposure and auto-white-balance "hunting" drift at about 0.1–1 Hz, which lies inside or next to the passband, so EVM amplifies it into whole-frame pulsing. rPPG takes the same drift as signal. Turning these off from OpenCV on Windows is unreliable. On Windows 11 with OpenCV 4.6, setting `CAP_PROP_AUTO_EXPOSURE` to 0.25 had no effect and exposure read back -1 ([OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/exposure-setting-issue-with-opencv-in-python-microscope-dino-lite/10174)). On another Windows 10 setup, DSHOW returned -1 while MSMF accepted the call but always read back 0 ([OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/set-camera-paramter-without-reset-of-it/15021)). `CAP_PROP_AUTO_WB` was not implemented in either backend as of 4.5.1. The issue was closed with the comment that "nearly all videoio properties are optional and backend-specific" ([opencv#19621](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/issues/19621)). DirectShow exposure is on a log2-seconds scale, so -6 is about 15.6 ms and each step doubles or halves the time ([Kurokesu](https://www.kurokesu.com/main/2020/05/22/uvc-camera-exposure-timing-in-opencv/)). `cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_SETTINGS, 1)` opens the driver's own dialog, and **only the DSHOW backend supports it** ([OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/change-exposure-with-opencv-linux/10647/9)). That makes it the most reliable hackathon fallback: open the dialog once, untick auto exposure and auto white balance, and set the powerline frequency to 50 Hz. MSMF can also take seconds to minutes to open. DSHOW avoids that, and setting `OPENCV_VIDEOIO_MSMF_ENABLE_HW_TRANSFORMS=0` before opening helps if you stay on MSMF ([OpenCV forum](https://forum.opencv.org/t/using-opencv4-7-0-cap-open-0-to-open-the-camera-is-slow/13954)).

The "30 fps lock" is not a lock either. In low light, auto-exposure cameras commonly fall to about 15 fps. This figure comes from engineering experience, not a published source. A filter whose coefficients assume 30 fps then shifts its band upward: at 15 fps, the r_hi tuned for 2 Hz behaves like roughly 4 Hz. Live EVM projects point out that the band depends on the *capture* rate, and that capture falls to the processing rate if old frames are not dropped ([MattW2/Live-Video-Magnification](https://github.com/MattW2/Live-Video-Magnification)). The fixes are the same for both pipelines. Open with `CAP_DSHOW`, request MJPG at 640x480 at 30 fps (set FOURCC first), and read every property back. Run capture in a thread that keeps only the newest frame. Timestamp every frame with `perf_counter`. Then recompute the IIR coefficients from the real dt for EVM, and resample with `np.interp` onto a uniform 30 Hz grid for rPPG.

Mains flicker is the other hazard. Camera frame rate beating against light flicker can land near heart-rate frequencies, and camera anti-banding can itself suppress the rPPG signal ([Mironenko et al. CVPRW 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.12695)). Aliases are hard to predict, so fixed notch filters work poorly ([US 12039764](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12039764)). UK lighting flickers at 100 Hz. At exactly 30 fps that aliases to 10 Hz, outside the band, but small drifts in fps move the alias. Daylight or non-PWM LED light, the driver's 50 Hz option, and suspicion of any unnaturally stable spectral line are the defences. Compression matters too. The MMPD dataset, stored heavily compressed at 320x240, shows about three times the error of uncompressed lab datasets ([MMPD](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.03840)). Coarse pooling hides some of the codec's block noise.

## POS on three skin patches gets 4 bpm in the lab and 8–12 in the wild

The state of the art for an unsupervised, CPU-only rPPG pipeline is POS (Wang et al. 2017), with CHROM close behind. GREEN-only is not competitive. In the rPPG-Toolbox benchmark, POS scored 4.00 / 3.67 / 4.51 / 12.36 bpm MAE on UBFC-rPPG / PURE / UBFC-Phys / MMPD. CHROM scored 3.98 / 5.77 / 4.49 / 13.66, and GREEN scored 19.81 / 10.09 / 13.55 / 21.68 ([rPPG-Toolbox, NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.00716v3)). An independent comparison also found POS and CHROM the most robust ([PMC9604655](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9604655)). Supervised deep models beat both on darker skin ([arXiv 2404.05003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.05003v1.pdf)), but they generalise poorly across datasets ([MMPD](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.03840)) and are not worth the hackathon risk.

The reference POS implementation is short. Use a window of l = ceil(1.6·fs), which is 48 frames at 30 fps. In each window, divide RGB by its temporal mean and project with P = [[0,1,−1],[−2,1,1]]. Combine the two rows as h = S0 + (σ(S0)/σ(S1))·S1, subtract the mean, and overlap-add into the output. Then apply smoothness-priors detrending with λ = 100 and a Butterworth bandpass ([POS_WANG.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ubicomplab/rPPG-Toolbox/main/unsupervised_methods/methods/POS_WANG.py)). CHROM uses the same 1.6 s window with Xs = 3R − 2G, Ys = 1.5R + G − 1.5B, a 3rd-order 0.7–2.5 Hz bandpass, S = Xf − αYf, and Hann-windowed overlap-add ([CHROME_DEHAAN.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ubicomplab/rPPG-Toolbox/main/unsupervised_methods/methods/CHROME_DEHAAN.py)). The detrend computes (I − (I + λ²DᵀD)⁻¹)x. λ is squared in the code, and the inverse can be precomputed once for a fixed 300-sample window. The toolbox evaluates with a **2nd-order Butterworth at 0.75–2.5 Hz** (its code default is 1st order, 0.6–3.3 Hz). The authors warn that filter choice "can substantially affect heart rate errors" ([post_process.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ubicomplab/rPPG-Toolbox/main/evaluation/post_process.py); [arXiv 2210.00716](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.00716v3)). For a seated card player, 45–150 bpm is the right band. Keep it identical between offline validation and live use.

For the ROI, the toolbox reproduces its published numbers with nothing more than a smoothed Haar face box ([rPPG-Toolbox](https://github.com/ubicomplab/rPPG-Toolbox)), so a precise ROI is a refinement, not a prerequisite. MediaPipe Face Landmarker excludes hair, eyes and background, and a Durham study found MediaPipe cheek ROIs gave the best HR ([Durham](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1748767)). MediaPipe 1.2.2 ships win_amd64 wheels for Python 3.9–3.14 ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/mediapipe/)). Use the Tasks API, because the legacy `mp.solutions` namespace is gone in newer releases ([mediapipe#6204](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/6204)). The `face_landmarker.task` model file has to be downloaded separately. pyVHR's landmark sets are ready to use: the forehead set is `[10, 67, 69, 104, 108, 109, 151, 299, 337, 338]`, and its high-priority ROIs are the forehead centre plus both lower cheeks, e.g. left `[215, 138, 135, 210, 212, 57, 216, 207, 192]` ([pyVHR utils.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/phuselab/pyVHR/master/pyVHR/extraction/utils.py)). Fill each polygon with `cv2.fillConvexPoly` and average the skin pixels.

For heart-rate estimation, the toolbox zero-pads to the next power of two, takes a periodogram, and returns 60 × the in-band peak frequency ([post_process.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ubicomplab/rPPG-Toolbox/main/evaluation/post_process.py)). Live, use a 10 s window (300 samples) updated every 0.5–1 s. A 10 s window has a native bin of 6 bpm. Zero-padding to 2048 points gives about 0.9 bpm bins, and parabolic interpolation refines the peak further. Welch (nperseg about 256 on a 15–20 s buffer) lowers variance at the cost of latency. No head-to-head webcam comparison of FFT and Welch exists, so a plain Hann-windowed FFT is the simpler default. For signal quality, use the toolbox's de Haan-style SNR: power within ±6 bpm of f0 and 2f0, divided by the remaining in-band power, in dB ([post_process.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ubicomplab/rPPG-Toolbox/main/evaluation/post_process.py)). No published "no reading" threshold exists. A defensible starting point is to show nothing when SNR stays below about 0 dB for 2 s, when the face is lost or moving, or when the buffer holds less than 6–8 s. On top of that, smooth with a median of the last 5 estimates, track within ±15 bpm, and check whether the half-frequency peak is the real fundamental when the second harmonic dominates. These are heuristics to tune on the camera.

Realistic accuracy depends on conditions more than on algorithm choice. On MMPD (30 fps mobile video, Fitzpatrick types 3–6), POS MAE was **5.76 bpm for skin type 3 but 11–13 for types 5–6**, 9.70 stationary, 17.05 walking, 7.26 under bright LED, and 10.71 under natural light ([MMPD](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.03840)). UBFC-rPPG, where 4 bpm is achievable, was recorded on a Logitech C920 at 640x480, 30 fps, with mostly light-skinned, still subjects ([MMPD paper](https://ubicomplab.cs.washington.edu/pdfs/mmpd.pdf)). For a still, seated player under even LED light, plan for **about 3–8 bpm once the window fills, and 10–15 bpm or worse** with talking, head turns, dim light or darker skin. No laptop-webcam-specific study confirms this range, so validate on a UBFC video offline first, then against a fingertip oximeter on the team.

## Physiology tracks arousal, and arousal does not distinguish a bluff from a strong hand

Seventy years of deception research gives the demo its honest frame. DePaulo et al.'s meta-analysis of 158 cues across 116 studies found a median cue effect of |d| = 0.10. Among the larger nonverbal cues, nervousness/tension reached only about d = 0.27 ([PMC10884279](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10884279); [typeset.io](https://typeset.io/pdf/moderators-of-nonverbal-indicators-of-deception-a-meta-3kfqtv8271.pdf)). Bond and DePaulo's pooled analysis of 24,483 judges put lie-detection accuracy at **about 54%**, and experts did no better than laypeople (d = −0.03) ([PubMed 16859438](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/)). Luke (2019) argues that even these small effects are inflated by flexible coding and low power, and that wholly useless cues could have produced the existing literature ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691619838258/); [PMC7767987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7767987)). The NRC's polygraph review is the decisive statement for a physiological demo. It found the theory "quite weak", noted that arousal varies widely between people and has many causes besides deception, and expected that more investment would bring "only modest improvements" ([NAP](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10420/chapter/2)). A webcam is a noisier sensor than a contact polygraph, so any per-hand "BLUFF" verdict would claim more than the strongest instruments can deliver.

Heart rate responds clearly to stress, but stress is not lying. Social-evaluative stress raises HR with large effects, about ES 0.85–0.89 in TSST meta-analyses ([Seddon et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739033); [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/337699)). That response builds over minutes. In the Concealed Information Test, recognition of a relevant item produces HR effects of d* = 0.89, but often as a *deceleration* (skin conductance d* = 1.55 is stronger, though a webcam cannot measure it) ([Meijer et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24916920)). Webcam stress classification on the UBFC-Phys TSST-like protocol reached 85.48% for binary stress from remote pulse-rate-variability features, but only 63% for three classes ([Meziati Sabour et al. 2023](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Meziati_Sabour_et_al._2023.pdf)). No peer-reviewed webcam rPPG *deception* study was found. Poker evidence points the same way. In Slepian et al. (2013), novices judging World Series clips were at or below chance from faces and best from arm movements ([APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/to-call-a-players-poker-hand-look-to-the-arms-not-the-face.html)). Lee's 71-player thesis found that bluffs produced skin-conductance responses *similar* to truthful strong bets, and that wins aroused players more than losses ([UWSpace](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/7500)). Small studies report HR and electrodermal activity rising during bluffs ([Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/wctp-25/126023792); [MIT LiveNet](https://hd.media.mit.edu/livenet/poker.php)), but none is large or replicated.

| Signal | Webcam-measurable? | What the evidence supports | Demo use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (rPPG) | Yes, about 5–10 bpm MAE | Large effect for sustained stress; indirect and bidirectional for deception | Primary: smoothed, baseline-relative, with quality shown |
| Blink rate (EAR) | Yes, robust at 30 fps | Suppression under cognitive load during lying ([Leal & Vrij 2008](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10919-008-0051-0)); rebound not replicated ([Perelman 2014](https://peerj.com/articles/260)) | Primary: blinks/min vs baseline |
| Pupil size | Not reliably on RGB ([arXiv 2407.11204](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.11204v1)) | Good only with lab/IR eye-tracking | Do not use |
| Gaze aversion | Yes | No evidence; a worldwide folk belief ([PMC10884279](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10884279)) | Myth-busting slide only |
| Micro-expressions | No | "No scientific support"; METT training did not help ([Jordan et al. 2019](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kassin/files/Jordan%20et%20al%20(2019)%20-%20METT.pdf)) | Never claim |

Blink detection pairs well with rPPG. Eye aspect ratio (EAR) from eye landmarks stays roughly constant while the eye is open and drops toward zero in a blink, with a threshold near 0.2 ([Soukupová & Čech 2016](https://vision.fe.uni-lj.si/cvww2016/proceedings/papers/05.pdf)). It comes almost free from the same Face Landmarker call. The honest protocol follows from the NRC's point about individual variation. Every number must be relative to the player's own baseline. Start with a signal check, then about 60 s of resting baseline, then a "talking" baseline, since speech itself changes blinks and rPPG quality. An optional "pick a card" round adapts the CIT: the player denies all five cards, and the app reports which card produced the biggest relative response, framed as recognition, with 20% chance odds stated. Then come poker hands, shown as z-scores against baseline. The strongest moment is to reveal the cards *after* the hand and plot arousal against both hand strength and bluff status, so the audience sees for themselves that a monster hand and a bluff can look the same.

The legal framing points the same way. EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) has banned AI that infers emotions in workplaces and education institutions since 2 February 2025. The Commission's guidelines extend this to inferring intentions, and emotion recognition elsewhere is high-risk ([FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/); [Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/-/media/resource-pages/ai-monitor/guidance/eu-commission-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-established-by-the-ai-act.pdf?la=en)). A voluntary entertainment demo is not clearly inside the ban, and the UK is not bound by the Act. Still, a university hackathon sits close enough to "education" that the safe design is to display raw physiological signals, not inferred emotions or honesty. Make it opt-in with a visible camera indicator, process locally, store nothing, and never run it on passers-by, staff or judges. Wording such as "Pulse (est.)", "Blinks/min vs your baseline", "Arousal meter" and "Signal quality", with a footer reading "For fun. Not a lie detector. Physiological arousal has many causes.", is both accurate and defensible. Avoid "lie detector", "bluff score", "truth score", and any accuracy figure the team has not measured itself.

## Conclusion

The three questions turn out to have one answer. The same weak, slow, baseline-dependent physiological signal limits all three parts. EVM needs coarse pooling and seconds of settling. rPPG needs a 10 s window and still carries errors as large as the effects it is meant to show. The deception literature says that even a perfect pulse would only report arousal. This makes the engineering priorities clear. Locking the camera (DSHOW, the settings dialog, measured timestamps) and gating on signal quality matter more than choosing between POS and CHROM or between DoLP and Butterworth. Showing "no reading" honestly is a feature, not a failure.

For the pitch, the debunking is the novelty. Most "lie detector" hackathon projects overclaim. A demo that shows a visibly throbbing face, a credible pulse estimate with a confidence gauge, and then the reveal that the bluff and the full house spiked identically teaches the real science of tells. It is also robust to the very failures that would sink a "bluff detector". The open empirical gaps are a laptop-webcam MAE per skin tone, the hit rate of a webcam CIT, and fixed-point word lengths for a hardware port. The team can measure these at the event, and they would make a credible "what we found" slide.
